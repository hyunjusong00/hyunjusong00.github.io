> This is a coursework case study from Kookmin's AI for Business track at the Global PBL Program in Irvine. RenTech is treated here as a hypothetical client. The proposal scenario, financials, and architecture decisions are mine.

## The problem

Renaissance Technologies (RenTech) is a $33B AUM quantitative hedge fund. About 350 employees, petabyte-scale historical market data, and a research culture that runs heavy backtests at the end of every quarter.

The capacity problem is shape, not size. Their on-prem GPU cluster (around 150 to 200 DGX H100 units) runs at roughly 30% utilization for most of the year. Then at quarter-end, every researcher tries to test new strategies at the same time, and the cluster becomes the bottleneck.

Moving everything to cloud sounds like the obvious answer. It isn't. For a firm whose live trading depends on microsecond decisions, you can't lift production execution to a shared infrastructure. Latency on live orders, custody of proprietary alpha, and integrity of execution are all reasons to keep certain workloads on dedicated hardware in their own data center.

The actual question is which workloads cross the line.

## My role

Solutions Architect on a 4-person AWS sales team simulation, pitching the migration plan to RenTech leadership (CTO, Head of Quant Research, Head of Infrastructure, CFO). I owned the architecture design and the cost model.

The proposal went through 7 iterations before landing on v7. The early versions over-committed to AWS. We tried to move kdb+ to AWS FinSpace, archive everything via Snowmobile, and assume RenTech would accept a near-total infrastructure rewrite. The later versions kept tightening the on-prem perimeter, until the question shifted from "can this move" to "should this move."

## Key decisions

### The hybrid boundary: backtests move, live trading stays

The line we drew:

- **On-prem**: live trading execution, model serving, proprietary alpha IP, low-latency networking. Speed and integrity are non-negotiable here. Microseconds matter. Data custody matters more.
- **AWS**: quarterly backtesting compute, large-scale model training, historical data storage.

Backtests are the right shape for cloud. Compute-heavy, time-bounded, and they don't talk to the live market. Burst once a quarter, idle for three months, burst again.

### Burst capacity instead of fixed

This was the biggest cost lever in the model. Instead of buying enough H100s to handle quarter-end peak (which leaves them mostly idle for nine months of the year), we scaled from 20 GPUs as baseline up to 200 at quarter-end on p5.48xlarge. Same peak compute available. No idle hardware bill in the off-quarter.

CPU side, the same logic. c7i.48xlarge scaled from 100 to 500 units, validated against the existing Xeon 8480+ for behavioral equivalence so the existing models wouldn't drift.

### Storage tiering, not lift-and-shift

Historical market data is around 25 PB. Most of it is rarely touched. S3 with three tiers cleared this:

- **Standard** for the current and prior quarter
- **Infrequent Access** for the prior 2 to 3 years
- **Glacier Deep Archive** for everything older, including compliance retention

The initial transfer compressed down to roughly 40 TB and moved over Direct Connect 100 Gbps × 2 (dual circuit, no single-link failure mode). After that, ongoing replication is incremental and the AWS side never has to re-read the original archive.

### What got cut between v5 and v7

AWS deprecated both FinSpace and Snowmobile by the time we finalized v7. Snowball Edge was also blocked by RenTech security review (no physical-device ingress to their data center).

That left us with two real constraints:

- **No managed kdb+.** We replaced FinSpace's managed runtime with self-managed kdb+ on EC2 backed by S3. RenTech's kdb+ deployment is heavily customized anyway (internal indexing, proprietary loaders, alpha-specific schemas), so a managed service would have hit operational friction even if it had survived.
- **No physical data transfer.** Snowmobile and Snowball Edge are both off the table. The 40 TB compressed pipe over Direct Connect is the only path in.

Both cuts simplified the proposal. Fewer moving parts, fewer vendor dependencies, fewer surfaces RenTech's security team has to approve.

## What the v7 plan looks like

| Category | Before (all on-prem) | After (hybrid) |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual infrastructure cost** | $36M / year | $25.5M / year (−29%) |
| **Storage** | NVMe / HDD / Tape, 25 PB+ | S3 3-tier (Standard, IA, Glacier Deep Archive) |
| **Time-series engine** | Self-hosted kdb+ cluster | kdb+ on EC2 + S3 (self-managed) |
| **GPU** | DGX H100, 150 to 200 units, ~30% utilization | p5.48xlarge, 20 → 200 (quarter-end burst) |
| **CPU** | Xeon 8480+, 800 to 1,200 units | c7i.48xlarge, 100 → 500 (quarter-end burst) |
| **High-memory** | 2 to 4 TB RAM servers, 30 to 50 units | r7iz, 5 → 20 units (1 TB RAM, large jobs split) |
| **Network** | InfiniBand 200 Gbps internal | Direct Connect 100 Gbps × 2, ~40 TB compressed initial transfer |
| **Sensitive workload exposure** | On-prem only | On-prem only (unchanged) |

Cost split after migration: $15M on-prem (live trading + IP-sensitive workloads) + $10.5M AWS (backtest compute + storage). Total $25.5M.

## What this taught me

The thing I underestimated going in was how much of solutions architecture is actually about *what stays out of scope*. v1 wanted to move everything. v7 keeps the cloud perimeter tight on purpose. The CFO question (cost) and the CTO question (risk) only meet in the middle when the architecture knows exactly where to draw the line.

Coming from product work, the parallel was clearer than I expected. Good product work cuts features that don't earn their place. Good cloud architecture cuts workloads that don't earn the migration. Both jobs are about resisting the urge to include everything.
