## Overview

Sellii is an AI-assisted reseller tool designed to help users discover profitable listings across online marketplaces and reduce repetitive sourcing work.

The project started from a common reseller problem. Finding good deals requires constantly checking multiple marketplaces, comparing resale prices manually, and reacting quickly before listings disappear.

Most existing tools only solve part of the workflow. Some send listing alerts without evaluating profitability, while others help with price research but still require manual searching and listing management.

Sellii was designed to connect those steps into a single workflow supported by an AI agent.

## The problem

For many resellers, sourcing inventory is both time-sensitive and repetitive.

Users often refresh platforms such as Facebook Marketplace, OfferUp, and Craigslist throughout the day looking for underpriced items. Even after finding a listing, they still need to:

- compare resale prices
- estimate profit margins
- contact sellers
- create resale listings manually

This creates a workflow with high effort and inconsistent results.

Our goal was to reduce that friction while still keeping users in control of important actions.

## Key product decisions

### 1. Three-level automation system

One of the biggest UX challenges with AI agents is trust.

Most users are not comfortable giving an AI full control immediately, especially when real money and marketplace accounts are involved. Because of this, we designed Sellii around three automation levels:

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| Level 1 | Deal alerts only |
| Level 2 | AI-generated drafts for messages and listings requiring user approval |
| Level 3 | Fully automated messaging and listing actions |

The goal was to let users gradually increase automation as their trust in the system grew. Rather than treating automation as an all-or-nothing feature, we treated it as a progression.

### 2. Vision-based marketplace interaction

Instead of relying only on marketplace HTML structures, the agent was designed to operate more like a human user.

The system used:

- screenshots
- GPT-4o vision analysis
- tool calls for pricing and listing generation
- browser interaction patterns

This approach made the system more resilient to UI changes across marketplaces and allowed the agent to operate across multiple platforms with a more flexible workflow.

### 3. Marketing designed around user behavior

For the launch strategy, I focused less on technical AI explanations and more on showing how Sellii fits into a reseller's existing routine.

Instead of marketing the product as "advanced AI automation," the launch content demonstrated:

- how resellers normally search for deals
- where time gets wasted
- how Sellii reduces repetitive work

This made the product easier to understand and helped position the AI agent as a practical workflow tool rather than just a technical feature.

### 4. Brand identity focused on trust

We also spent significant time on branding because trust was central to the product experience.

The logo system was designed around mirrored "i" letterforms representing buyer and seller interaction. The visual direction aimed to make the product feel reliable, lightweight, and workflow-oriented rather than overly futuristic or experimental.

For an AI product handling marketplace actions, visual trust and clarity became important UX elements themselves.

## What I learned

The biggest lesson from Sellii was that trust is one of the most important parts of designing AI agents.

Users are often willing to accept AI assistance, but not immediate full automation. Designing gradual control and approval systems became just as important as the AI capabilities themselves.

I also learned that branding and marketing strongly affect how users perceive AI products. For tools involving automation, the product needs to feel understandable and predictable before users are willing to rely on it.

Overall, the project changed the way I think about AI UX. Good AI products are not only about capability. They are also about making users feel comfortable enough to adopt that capability into their daily workflow.
