## Overview

Trywepp is an AI-based platform designed to help founders evaluate startup ideas before building, while also giving early-stage investors visibility into ideas before they become formal companies.

The project started from a simple observation. Founders and investors are often looking for the same thing from opposite directions.

Founders want fast and structured feedback before spending months building a product. Investors want earlier access to promising ideas before they are fully packaged into pitch decks and fundraising rounds.

Most existing tools did not fully address this gap. AI idea validators often produced shallow scores without meaningful reasoning, while traditional consulting-style research was too slow and expensive for very early-stage ideas.

Trywepp was designed to create a middle layer between those two extremes.

## Early direction and pivot

The first version of Trywepp focused on peer feedback between student builders.

The original idea was that software students could review each other's startup ideas and provide useful validation before development. However, early retention data showed that the model was not producing meaningful engagement.

Out of the first 30 users, only a small number returned after the first week. Most feedback also lacked depth and focused more on encouragement than actual evaluation.

After several user conversations and discussions with early-stage investors, the product direction shifted toward a two-sided platform connecting founders and pre-seed investors.

This changed the product from "builders helping builders" to "structured early-stage signal for both founders and investors."

## Key product decisions

### 1. Pivoting to a two-sided market

One of the most important decisions was moving away from peer validation and focusing on founders and investors instead.

The shift happened after:

- low early retention
- repeated user feedback
- conversations with angel investors
- clearer understanding of pre-seed workflows

The new structure created stronger incentives on both sides:

- founders submitted ideas for meaningful evaluation and visibility
- investors gained access to earlier deal flow

### 2. Simplifying the evaluation framework

The first version of the AI evaluation system used eight evaluation agents. Over time, the framework was simplified into six core dimensions:

- market size
- problem urgency
- timing
- product quality
- defensibility
- business model

This decision came after researching how firms such as Y Combinator and a16z evaluate early-stage startups.

Reducing the number of evaluation categories improved clarity and reduced overlap between scoring dimensions.

### 3. Rebuilding the AI analysis pipeline for speed

The original evaluation pipeline used a three-step structure for every agent:

- Draft
- Skeptic
- Judge

While this produced detailed outputs, total response time exceeded 50 seconds.

The pipeline was later redesigned into a lighter two-step structure:

- Draft
- Judge (including internal critique)

Additional optimizations included:

- parallelized external search retrieval
- prompt caching
- removal of redundant quality-check layers

This reduced average report generation time to approximately 30 seconds while maintaining analytical depth.

### 4. Building frontend components directly

During the project, I began contributing directly to the frontend using React and Next.js.

This changed the product workflow significantly because product decisions could immediately be tested in implementation instead of passing through multiple revision cycles.

It also made prioritization more practical. Features were evaluated not only based on ideas, but also based on implementation cost, usability, and long-term clarity.

## What I learned

The biggest lesson from Trywepp was how important user clarity is in product decisions.

At the beginning, many features and evaluation categories seemed useful in theory. But after talking directly with users and investors, it became much clearer which parts actually created value and which added unnecessary complexity.

I also learned that working directly in the codebase changes how product decisions are made. Contributing to implementation made prioritization more realistic and helped reduce communication gaps inside the team.

Finally, the project taught me that two-sided products depend heavily on sequencing. In marketplaces, solving the order of adoption is often more important than adding more features.
