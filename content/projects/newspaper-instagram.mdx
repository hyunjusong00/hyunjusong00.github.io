## Overview

When I became Editor-in-Chief of Kookmin University Newspaper, the Instagram account mainly functioned as a place to repost finished articles.

Most posts were static images that simply repurposed print headlines. Engagement was low, growth had slowed, and most followers were already existing readers from campus.

We decided to rebuild the account into an actual social media brand. That meant designing the feed itself as a product: a system of seasonal banners, story teasers, event posters, and Reels that all read as one publication.

The goal was less about chasing virality and more about giving the newspaper a visual identity strong enough that students recognized it on the feed before they read the headline.

## The problem

The newspaper already had strong written content, but the way we presented it on Instagram had several limitations:

- static reposts of print headlines, with no platform-native format
- low engagement and slow follower growth
- no consistent visual identity across the feed
- limited reach outside readers who already knew the paper
- a student design team comfortable with print layout but less practiced in social and short-form video

The goal was not just to "post more often." It was to redesign how editorial content showed up on a social platform, both as banners and posters and as Reels.

## My role

As Editor-in-Chief, I led the overall content and editorial direction for the Instagram account.

My responsibilities included:

- deciding which stories became feed banners, posters, or Reels
- defining the seasonal visual identity for the feed
- creating production briefs for banner and Reel pieces
- coordinating editorial and design teams
- structuring both the static-design and Reels workflows
- integrating Instagram production into the existing weekly publication process

Rather than directly designing or filming, I focused on building a system that allowed the design team to execute consistently and independently across all formats.

## Key decisions

### 1. Designing the feed as a system, not as individual posts

The biggest shift was treating the feed itself as a designed product.

Instead of approving posts one at a time, we built a banner and poster system with shared rules across the entire account:

- a consistent grid logic so adjacent posts read together as a row
- a recurring color palette per season
- shared type hierarchy across banner types (story teaser, event poster, Reel cover)
- a fixed set of layout templates the design team could reuse

This is what made the live feed read as one brand even though dozens of student designers contributed to it across a semester.

### 2. Treating every post as editorial content, not promotion

Whether it was a banner, an event poster, or a Reel, every post was planned the same way an article was:

- topic selection
- editorial angle
- structure
- intended audience reaction
- publishing schedule

That single decision is what let static posters (학위수여식 announcements, K-star interview promos, Olympic series banners) sit on the feed next to Reels without feeling off-brand.

### 3. Brief-first for banners and posters, script-first for Reels

The production workflow was different for each format, but the principle was the same: validate the idea on paper before producing.

- **Banners and posters** started from a one-page brief: angle, headline, visual references, and layout slot in the feed grid.
- **Reels** started from a short written script: hook structure, narrative flow, approximate timing, visual direction.

This filtered out weak ideas early and made the actual production time much shorter, which mattered because the design team was made up of students balancing coursework and weekly print deadlines.

### 4. Prioritizing feed consistency over viral spikes

Rather than chasing a single viral post, we focused on maintaining a steady publishing rhythm and a consistent look across the entire feed.

Repeated exposure to a coherent identity gradually changed how students recognized the account. Over the semester, that consistency improved watch time, repeat engagement, and follower growth more reliably than isolated high-performing posts.

## What we built

### Editorial workflow

- briefing system for banners, posters, and Reels
- script templates for different Reel formats
- editorial calendar integrating both static and video content
- named-editor ownership structure per post

### Content formats

- **Weekly story banners** introducing headline pieces from the print issue
- **Event posters** for graduations (학위수여식), business school OT, conferences, K-star interviews, Olympic series, and recruitment cycles
- **Issue cover banners** announcing each new Vol. release
- **Campus interview Reels** with students and faculty
- **Short explainer Reels** on news and policy topics
- **Behind-the-scenes coverage** of the newsroom and events

### Team workflow

- structured editorial-to-design handoff for each format
- reusable banner and poster templates
- weekly publishing cadence across banners, posters, and Reels

## Results

- Instagram followers grew approximately **1.5× within one semester**
- Around 200 new followers were gained through the combined banner and Reel strategy
- The feed became visually coherent across seasons, which was reflected in steady engagement and watch time growth
- The design team expanded from print-only work into a full feed-design and short-form video practice

More importantly, the newspaper developed a repeatable visual identity and production system that future editorial teams could continue building on.

## What I learned

The biggest lesson from this experience was that on a social platform, the feed is the product, not the individual post.

Simply reposting existing content rarely works. The format itself, and the way posts sit next to each other in a grid, changes how stories need to be structured and presented.

I also learned that strong creative direction is often about designing good systems rather than controlling every detail. Clear briefs, consistent templates, and workflow handoffs helped the team produce better results across both static and video formats than overly detailed supervision would have.

Finally, I learned how quickly creative teams can grow once they trust the system. By the end of the semester, the team was proposing new banner formats and Reel ideas on their own, rather than waiting for direction.
