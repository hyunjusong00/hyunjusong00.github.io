Before working on startup products, I spent a year leading a 30+ person student newsroom at Kookmin University Newspaper. Looking back, it was the first time I experienced many of the same challenges that appear in product work: outdated systems, unclear workflows, cross-functional collaboration, and the need to redesign both internal processes and user-facing experiences at the same time.

At the time, I did not have a "PM" title. But many of the ways I think about products today started there.

## Starting point

I originally joined the newspaper as part of the design team.

The organization itself was much larger and more structured than many people expected. We operated on weekly publication cycles with student reporters, editors, designers, and leadership members all working together under tight deadlines.

Very quickly, I noticed two recurring problems.

First, the team structure was becoming more international. Korean and international students worked together with very different creative and communication styles. Without shared workflows or feedback systems, consistency depended too heavily on a small number of people reviewing everything manually.

Second, the internal tooling was outdated. Most collaboration still depended on a custom editing tool that had been built years earlier for a much smaller organization. While it technically worked, the workflow no longer matched how the newsroom actually operated.

At the same time, the newspaper website had not meaningfully evolved in years. The design team spent significant time creating visual identity systems for articles and print editions, but most of that work became nearly invisible once published online.

As a regular design team member, I could see these issues clearly but did not yet have the authority to change them.

## What changed after becoming Editor-in-Chief

When I became Editor-in-Chief, I knew there were many issues to address but limited time and organizational bandwidth. Instead of trying to solve everything at once, I focused on two major areas:

- improving internal collaboration workflows
- redesigning the newspaper's public-facing digital experience

### 1. Rebuilding internal communication and workflow

The first major change was replacing how the newsroom communicated and organized work internally.

Rather than relying entirely on older tools and fragmented messaging, we introduced:

- Notion for structured collaboration
- KakaoWork for real-time communication
- clearer ownership systems
- centralized editorial calendars and archives

At first, these changes seemed operational rather than strategic. But over time, they significantly changed how the team functioned.

Communication became more transparent, responsibilities became clearer, and many small misunderstandings disappeared because information was no longer scattered across personal chats and disconnected systems.

One important lesson from this experience was realizing that tools shape team behavior much more than people initially expect. Workflow systems are not only logistical decisions. They influence collaboration culture itself.

### 2. Redesigning the newspaper website

The second major project was redesigning the newspaper website.

The original website treated articles primarily as blocks of text organized by rigid category structures. Readers had difficulty browsing naturally, and the visual work created by the design team was barely reflected online.

The redesign focused on:

- issue-based browsing
- stronger visual hierarchy
- editorial-style layouts
- mobile-first reading experience
- improved integration of design and storytelling

I worked closely with the university IT department and information administration team throughout the process. Because the website existed inside the university's broader infrastructure, many design decisions also required technical and institutional coordination.

This was the first time I experienced how much cross-functional work large redesign projects actually require. The design decisions themselves were often easier than aligning stakeholders, technical constraints, and organizational priorities.

## Expanding into video content

At the same time, the newspaper also began shifting more heavily into short-form video content.

Previously, most stories existed only as written articles. We started experimenting with Instagram Reels as a parallel editorial format rather than treating social media as only a promotion channel.

The design team began producing:

- short-form interviews
- campus explainers
- behind-the-scenes coverage
- visual storytelling content

This helped the newspaper reach students who were unlikely to discover long-form articles directly through the website alone.

More importantly, it expanded the role of the design team from a supporting production function into an active publishing team.

## Results

Within one semester:

- website traffic increased by **over 40%**
- Instagram followers grew approximately **1.5×**
- overall student engagement improved noticeably
- feedback from faculty and students became significantly more positive

Internally, team morale also improved because the systems surrounding the work finally reflected the quality of the work itself.

## What I learned

Looking back, this experience shaped many of the ways I now think about products and teams.

### Systems shape behavior

Changing collaboration tools affected not only workflow efficiency but also how people communicated and worked together. Small operational systems can create large cultural effects over time.

### Visibility matters

Even strong work loses impact when it is presented through weak surfaces. Sometimes the highest-leverage improvement is not changing the work itself, but redesigning the environment where users experience it.

### Cross-functional teams need shared structure, not identical working styles

The newsroom became more effective not because everyone worked the same way, but because people had clearer handoff systems and shared expectations between teams.

### Organizational work is often the hardest part

The most difficult parts of these projects were rarely visual or creative decisions. Coordination, stakeholder communication, and institutional alignment were usually the real bottlenecks.

At the time, I did not think of this experience as "product management." But many of the product instincts I rely on today, like systems thinking, workflow design, prioritization, and cross-functional coordination, started in that newsroom.
